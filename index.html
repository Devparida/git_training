CHAPTER 02IXIGO TRAINS

Confident Compare

When a preferred train comes back waitlisted, ixigo shows a number and stops. Only 26% of travellers resolve their journey within twenty-four hours — against a base of 104M annual train bookings and ₹511 Cr in train revenue.

01 — Executive summary

Converting intent, not creating it

BookMyShow is India's dominant entertainment ticketing platform — 50M+ downloads, 15M+ tickets a month, ₹1,869 crore FY25 total income. But it converts intent; it does not create it. The ₹13,000 crore live events market is growing, and users who arrive without a plan are being served by Instagram, YouTube, WhatsApp groups and Zomato District instead.

Among users who begin a discovery-led session and view at least three listings, only 18% complete a booking within 7 days. Primary research points at a decision problem rather than a discovery problem: users can find events, but they cannot commit to one.

The recommendation

Ship a three-part decision layer on top of the existing catalogue in six weeks: a "My Plans" shortlist workspace to save, folder and compare events instead of holding twenty options in your head; an all-in price display showing the fee-inclusive total at the point of shortlisting rather than the point of payment; and shareable shortlists with group voting, so friends vote in a browser with no app install and the group decision stays inside BookMyShow.

Land and expand

Rather than a big-bang release to 50M+ users, a phased cohort approach validates conversion lift at each stage before scaling.

The growth loop

Browse & save → share the shortlist → friends vote, no install → group decides → group books together → friends become users.

Each share puts BookMyShow in front of three to five people at no acquisition cost, and those people have already expressed intent — they are warm, not cold. At least 39.1% of users already coordinate plans in WhatsApp; the loop leverages that existing behaviour rather than asking anyone to change habits.

What I consciously chose not to do

A shortlist workspace over an AI recommendation feed

The "Plan For You" feed scores respectably and sounds impressive, but it needs a recommendation engine, preference modelling and a data pipeline we do not have — and it solves the wrong problem. Users are not failing to find events; they are failing to choose between the events they found. The sacrifice: no immediate lift in events-viewed-per-session. We may see fewer. That is acceptable — we are optimising for conversion, not engagement theatre.

Shareable links over in-app group chat

An in-app thread would keep everything inside BookMyShow, but group chat is a product in itself — moderation, real-time infrastructure, media handling — a six-month build, not a six-week MVP. The sacrifice: we lose visibility into what friends actually say. Valuable, but not worth four months of delay.

No-login voting over authenticated voting

Friends vote without an account, which costs us their phone number, city and preferences. It is a leaky funnel by design. The sacrifice: less acquisition data — but requiring login would collapse the vote rate. Asking someone to install a 40MB app to cast one vote is not a reasonable trade in a market where phone storage is scarce.

Three travel-decision personas

On provenance

These personas are synthesised from response clusters across Q2 and Q4–Q8. Segment sizes are directional — mapped from related survey questions, not a formal cluster analysis. The quotes are representative statements built from response patterns, not verbatim, because the fielded survey was closed-ended.

The Certainty Seeker · ≈29%

Wants one thing above all: a seat that's actually going to happen. Will pay more for it, and won't gamble on a discount if there's real doubt attached.

"I'd rather pay ₹300 more and know I'm getting on that train than save money and spend the next two days checking my PNR."

Blocked by low trust in confirmation odds — 58% distrust an 82% prediction (Q8). Converts when a confirmed alternative is shown clearly, even at a premium — 52% pick the guaranteed option (Q5). Needs certainty ranked and surfaced first, not buried in a probability.

The Mode-Flexible Traveller · ≈40%

Cares about reaching the destination, not the train specifically. Once it's clear the preferred train isn't happening, switching mode is the obvious move — not a last resort.

"If the train's not confirmed, just show me the flight or bus that gets me there on time — I don't need it to be a train."

Blocked by cross-mode options living in separate flows, so switching means starting a whole new search. Converts when bus and flight alternatives appear inline with the failed train search — 75% would take this route when it's made easy (Q6). Needs one comparison view spanning all modes, not three separate apps.

The Constraint-Bound Planner · ≈25%

Not after any confirmed seat — it has to actually fit their day. A confirmed ticket that lands at 3am or from the wrong station isn't a real option.

"There were other trains, sure — just not at a time or station that actually worked for me."

Blocked by departure time and station convenience, recurring named difficulties in Q3. Converts when alternatives are filterable by timing and station, not just confirmation odds. Needs ixigo to mirror their own definition of a confident plan: similar travel time and budget fit (Q7).

02 — Problem framing

Where the 74 points go

When a traveller's preferred train is unavailable or waitlisted, they have no single place to see their real options — so they hop between five or more apps and websites just to compare. And even when ixigo does show a confirmation chance, most travellers don't trust it enough to act on it.

The result: even travellers who already know exactly where and when they want to travel often can't lock in a plan within a day.

Modelled, not measured

Only the two endpoints are hard numbers — 100% by definition, and 26% from the brief's stated baseline. The three intermediate stages are a directional model built from where survey respondents report friction (Q3, Q8, Q9), not session-level analytics.

Business impact — why this is the safest first build

The goal is a 16-point lift, 26% → 42%. Recovering even half of the 34-point drop at Comparison & Evaluation — about 17 points — already clears that bar on its own. The second leak, Decide & Commit at −20 points, is fixable from the same screen.

Worth doing because it compounds across a huge recurring base: roughly 104M train bookings a year, ₹511 Cr in train revenue, and 92% of surveyed travellers hitting this exact problem recently.

How this model works

It runs on ixigo's own disclosed FY26 numbers — 104.12M train bookings and ₹511 Cr train revenue — plus real survey results for how travellers choose between modes (Q6). Swap it for ixigo's real number and everything recalculates.

Risks, trade-offs & constraints

Four conscious trade-offs

Honest confidence, over maximum resolution rate

Gave up: some short-term booking volume that overstated confidence would have captured. Why: overstated confidence is the exact problem this project exists to fix — inflating labels to hit 42% faster would just rebuild the trust gap under a new name.

The waitlisted moment only, not general trip planning

Gave up: a broader, always-on comparison tool that could apply to any search. Why: a narrower target gives a sharper, more defensible ROI case — and it's the one moment the survey evidence is actually about.

Metro-to-metro trunk routes, first

Gave up: immediate coverage for smaller towns — 47% of respondents aren't in a large metro (Q10). Why: these routes have the deepest cross-modal inventory and the fastest-accumulating confidence data. The model needs to be right before it's everywhere.

Simple High/Medium/Low labels, over precise percentages

Gave up: some information richness — a 95%-likely "High" and a 75%-likely "High" look the same. Why: the bare-percentage version is the thing 58% of travellers said they don't trust. Comprehensibility was chosen deliberately over precision.

Constraints carried through

  • Inventory coverage. Compare's cross-modal promise only holds where AbhiBus and ConfirmTkt have real-time parity with the train product — this directly decides which routes launch first.
  • Pricing consistency across modes. A fair comparison needs all-in pricing shown the same way for train, bus and flight. If a partner API returns base fares inconsistently, the apples-to-apples promise breaks silently.
  • Historical outcome data per route. Confidence accuracy depends on enough past PNR outcomes. This is the real gating factor on rollout speed — not engineering capacity.
One assumption, named

Every number in this case study is grounded in real data except one: the 20% at-risk estimate is a reasoned guess, not confirmed by ixigo. The mode split and the ₹49.08 per-booking rate both come from disclosed figures or the survey.

Case study 03 · Green Jeeva

The paperwork was the product

Selling nutritional ingredients into five US states with no brand, no distribution and no local presence. The bottleneck wasn't price or lead time. It was a PDF.

Role
Business Development Manager
Market
US nutritional ingredients, B2B
Timeline
2020–2022
Outcome
$200K new-market revenue · $770K FY23 accounts
01 — The segment nobody was serving

Selling to the people who make other people's brands

The obvious buyers were supplement brands. They were also the most contested, the most price-sensitive, and the least interested in an unknown supplier from Bhubaneswar.

The segment underneath them was more interesting: contract manufacturers and private-label houses — VOX Nutrition, Uniwell Laboratories, GMP Laboratories, 4Excelsior. They buy in larger volumes, they reorder on a predictable cycle, and they compete on their own operational reliability rather than on brand. That makes them rational about switching suppliers in a way brands are not.

Targeting them produced $170K in new revenue from a segment that hadn't been worked, and the accounts stuck — $770K in FY23 relationship revenue across the book.

02 — The insight

Deals stalled at the same place every time

I started logging where conversations died. Not why buyers said no — where they went quiet. The answer was consistent and unglamorous: the formulation review.

A contract manufacturer can't commit to an ingredient until their QA team has the certificate of analysis, the allergen statement, the non-GMO documentation and the MSDS in hand. Every day those documents took to arrive was a day the buyer's project sat blocked — and blocked projects get re-scoped around whichever supplier answered fastest.

We were competitive on price and lead time and losing anyway, because we were treating compliance documents as post-sale admin. The buyer was treating them as the product spec.

The order wasn't won at the quote. It was won by whoever's COA reached the QA inbox before the review meeting.
03 — What changed

Treating documentation as part of the offer

01
Documents moved before the ask
COA, allergen statement, non-GMO certification and MSDS attached to the first substantive conversation rather than produced on request. It removed a round trip from every deal and signalled that we'd been through a QA review before.
02
Pre-built document sets per product line
Organic and non-organic powders, extract powders, probiotics and essential oils each got a maintained pack, so responding took minutes instead of a chase through the lab.
03
Logistics aligned to the buyer's calendar
Delivery turnaround planned against production runs rather than quoted generically — which is the same insight applied one step later in the journey.

Across five states this supported market entry worth $200K in new revenue, seven long-term contracts worth $300K, and $400K in closed revenue against a quota it exceeded by 95%.

04 — Why this is a product story

The friction was in the journey, not the offer

Nothing about the ingredient changed. What changed was noticing that the buying journey had a blocking step we'd classified as paperwork, and that the person we were selling to was not the person who could say yes.

That is the same move as case study 01 — the user's real constraint (arrival time, QA sign-off) is not the thing the product is organised around (train, price). Finding the constraint the customer is actually managing, and building the offer around that, is most of the job in both.

Where I'd push back on myself

I ran this on pattern recognition from maybe thirty deals, with no control. The documentation change coincided with me getting better at the pitch and with a market that was expanding anyway. I believe the mechanism — buyers told me directly — but I'd hold the attribution loosely, and today I'd have tried holding the documents back on a subset of deals to see whether the effect was real.

01 — The product

What it does

Founders decide whether to build something mostly on conviction. The useful version of "validation" — market sizing, competitor scan, persona, risk — is work most people skip because it takes days and they don't know where to start.

Idea Validator compresses that into a structured report. The user describes their idea, industry, target audience, geography, business model and the problem being solved. The model returns a scored analysis with the reasoning behind every number.

01
Describe the idea
A structured form rather than a chat box — industry, audience, geography, business model, problem. The structure is what makes the output comparable across ideas.
02
Get a scored report
A validation score out of 100 with a confidence band, plus nine analysis blocks — each one showing its reasoning, not just a verdict.
03
Save, compare, export
Reports persist. Multiple ideas can be compared side by side on score, market size, competition, risk and revenue potential — which is the feature that turns it from a toy into a decision tool.
The design brief I set myself

The AI had to behave like a combination of a Y Combinator partner, a startup mentor, a product strategist and a VC analyst — actionable, honest, structured, and never generic. The hard rule: always show the reasoning behind a score. A number without a reason is the failure mode for this entire category of product.

02 — The framework

How an idea gets scored

The scoring model is the part of this project I'd defend in an interview. Rather than asking a model for "a score out of 10," the report is built on five weighted dimensions with fixed proportions, so two ideas are scored on the same basis and the weighting itself can be argued with.

DimensionWeightWeightingWhy it carries this much
Problem severity25%A weak problem cannot be rescued by a good market or a clever business model.
Market size25%Ceiling on everything downstream. Weighted equally with the problem because a severe problem in a tiny market is a consulting job, not a startup.
Competition20%Matters, but a crowded market is a signal of demand as often as a barrier — so it sits below the first two.
Monetisation potential15%Deliberately lower. Business models get discovered later far more often than problems do.
Feasibility15%Lowest weight on purpose — feasibility is the input a founder has the most control over.

06 — Limits

What this doesn't have yet

Stated plainly, because it's the difference between a shipped product and a validated one — and this project is the former.

  • No user research. Unlike the BookMyShow and ixigo work, nothing here rests on evidence from founders. The problem is one I recognised, not one I measured.
  • The weights are mine, and untested. 25/25/20/15/15 is a defensible allocation, but no outcome data supports it. The honest test is whether ideas scoring above 90 go on to do better than ideas scoring 60 — and that would take years of tracking to know.
  • No usage data. The app is live but I have no retention, conversion or activation numbers to report, so there's nothing here about whether the paywall is in the right place.
  • The landing page carries placeholder marketing copy. The live demo shows founder counts, a satisfaction percentage and named testimonials — these are scaffold content from the build, not real users or real endorsements. No claim on this page rests on them.
  • The output inherits the model's blind spots. TAM estimates generated by a language model are plausible-sounding, not sourced. The product tells users the reasoning behind every score partly so they can catch this themselves.
What I'd do next

Put it in front of ten founders with ideas they've already decided on, and check whether the report agrees with the decision they made — and, where it disagrees, whether the founder finds the disagreement useful or wrong. That's the cheapest available test of whether the scoring framework tracks anything real.
